01 · What a contour is

Build the hill in layers

Your model is a stack of sheets of board, one sheet for every contour. Each sheet is cut along one line: the line where the ground is exactly at that height. That line is a contour. Move the slider to stack the sheets. Look down on the stack from above (right) and the edges of the sheets are the contour lines on the plan.

02 · Interval, scale, datum

How far apart, how high, measured from where

Three terms you'll find in the title block of every survey.

Contour interval is the height between neighbouring lines. On this survey it's 1 m. On a site plan, every fifth line is usually drawn heavier and labelled: these are index contours.

Scale turns height into board. At 1:200, 1 m is 5 mm, so a 1 m contour is one 5 mm layer. Change the scale and the right interval and board thickness change with it.

Datum is the zero that heights are measured from. On Irish maps, levels are given above Ordnance Datum at Malin Head (OD Malin), roughly mean sea level. GPS and drone surveys often measure from a different zero, the WGS 84 ellipsoid, so always state which datum you are using.

Spot heights are single measured levels: floor levels, road crowns, the top of a wall. Use them where contours are too far apart to say enough.

03 · Reading the ground

Patterns to recognise

Contours draw a small set of shapes over and over. Learn these six and you can read almost any site.

HillClosed rings, rising towards the middle.
ValleyThe V points uphill, upstream. Water runs down its centre.
Spur or ridgeThe V points downhill. Water runs away on both sides.
SaddleA low point between two highs. The red dot is the pass.
Steep and gentleClose lines mean steep ground; wide spacing means gentle.
TerraceA wide gap between tight bands: a level shelf, natural or made.

    05 · Water

    Water crosses contours at right angles

    Rain takes the steepest way down, so it always flows square to the contours, gathers in the valleys and leaves the ridges. The arrows show that on this survey.

    Follow the arrows and you can trace where rain gathers: arrows converging point into a valley line, arrows spreading apart run off a ridge or spur. Where they meet a watercourse, the stream is collecting the run-off from that slope.

    Water shaped most sites long before anyone built on them, and often decided where people built: mills, farms and roads follow the water and the dry ground beside it.

    For your design: trace where water arrives at your building. Anything built across a valley line or at the foot of a slope will collect run-off. Plan drainage, entrances and finished floor levels around it.

    06 · Building on a slope

    Cut, fill, or step

    Every building on sloping ground changes the contours around it. Your site plan should show both: existing contours as dashed lines, proposed contours as solid lines, with spot heights on new floors and paths.

    Look for both on your own site: a level shelf between tight bands of contours is often a platform someone cut and filled long ago, for a building, a yard or a road.
